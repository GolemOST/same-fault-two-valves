Same Fault, Two Valves

Two cooling-water valves start sticking at 08:00 on a simulated plant. A standard monitor raises an alarm on one within {{F14_ALARM_DELAY}} minutes. The other, it cannot tell apart from a normal day.

I wanted to see, with real numbers, how much of a plant's trouble shows up in its recorded data. So I ran an experiment on a public benchmark: a simulated chemical plant that process-control researchers have worked with since 1993. It records 41 measurements and 11 controller outputs every three minutes.

I gave it the same fault in two places: a cooling-water valve that sticks, once on the reactor and once on the condenser, both from 08:00. A standard monitor raised an alarm on the first within {{F14_ALARM_DELAY}} minutes. On the second, over the next forty hours, it flagged about as often as it does on a normal day.

01One plant, run twice

The data is a public dataset (Rieth and colleagues, 2017) of 21,000 simulated runs of the Tennessee Eastman process. I scored faults on its 48-hour test runs. In those, each faulty run has a twin: for the first eight hours it matches a normal run value for value. At 08:00 the fault switches on and the two days split. I checked this for all {{TWIN_RUNS_CHECKED}} faulty runs used here.

So each chart below shows the same day twice, with the fault and without it.

02A standard monitor, trained on normal running

I trained a standard monitor from the process-monitoring textbooks: principal component analysis (PCA) on all 52 recorded values, fitted to 250,000 samples of normal operation. It flags any sample that falls outside what normal looked like, at a 99% limit. Three flags in a row count as an alarm.

The three that moved most are the reactor cooling-water valve signal ({{F14_M1}}× its normal spread), reactor temperature ({{F14_M2}}×) and the reactor cooling-water outlet temperature ({{F14_M3}}×). 05Quiet is the right output, for the wrong reason

Researchers who sort these faults put the condenser valve among the controllable ones: the control system compensates and the process state barely moves. For faults like that, they write, a monitor's flag rate "should ideally be as low as" its false-alarm rate, so operators are not distracted (Sun and colleagues, 2020; they sort the faults from prior knowledge, on a different dataset from the same simulator).

So staying quiet is the right output here. PCA did not decide that, though. The same paper notes that PCA cannot tell whether a fault is controllable or not.

What this monitor lacks is concrete. The valve's real stem position is not one of the 52 recorded values, and the recorded signal for that valve, XMV(11), barely changes: its spread after 08:00 is {{F15_XMV11_RATIO}} times its twin's. Methods built for faults like this do better. Du and Du (2018) report detecting it, and two other faults most methods miss, from the same kind of process data.

06What a person on rounds would see

A sticking valve has a look: the stem holds, then jumps, instead of travelling smoothly. Someone standing at it at the right moment can see it.

The simulator has no people in it, so I wrote these two field notes myself, the way a round could plausibly record them. They are fiction, and so are the gold marks on the charts above, which sit at the minute each note was written.

08How this was done

  • Data. Rieth, Amsel, Tran and Cook (2017), Harvard Dataverse, public domain. The three files used were checksum-verified at download: 500 normal runs to train, 500 more to measure false alarms, and test runs 1 to 20 of each of the 20 faults.
  • Names. Recorded values, fault types and the short descriptions of faults 16 to 20 follow the model code in N. L. Ricker's Tennessee Eastman archive, which marks 16 to 20 as unknown. Where that code says the separator's cooling water, I say the condenser's, as Du and Du do.
  • Monitor. PCA on the 52 standardised values, {{COMPONENTS}} components ({{CPV}}% of the variance), Hotelling T² and SPE limits at 99%. A sample is flagged when either crosses its limit. An alarm is three flags in a row, timed at the third.
  • Measured. The flag rate on 480,000 normal samples, and the flag rate, alarm count and alarm delay for all 20 faults. Full table below.
  • Checked. A second implementation, written separately, reproduced every measured number quoted in the text. Changing the tuning (80, 90 or 95% of the variance, or a 99.9% limit) does not change the picture.
  • Tools. Python (numpy, pandas, scipy). The 837 MB R data file is read by a small streaming reader written for this, which matched a reference reader value for value on the two normal-running files. Charts are plain SVG. I wrote the pipeline with an AI coding assistant, to my specification; every number on the page comes from that code and its separate re-check.
  • Code. The whole pipeline is public on GitHub, with the steps to download the data and re-run every number yourself.
  • Not claimed. This is a simulation, not a plant, and it tests one kind of monitor. The field notes are fiction. Neither the dataset's authors nor Pacific Science & Engineering Group reviewed or endorse this page.

Take this onto shift

Pick the three control valves you would least like to see stick. For each one, ask: if it stuck tonight, which screen would show it? If the honest answer is none, the first sign will be what someone notices walking past.
